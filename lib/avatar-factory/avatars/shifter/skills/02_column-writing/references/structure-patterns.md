# Reference: Structure Patterns

## Column Structure Templates

Shifter columns use established structural patterns. This document defines each pattern, when to apply it, and its internal architecture.

---

### Pattern 1: Inverted Pyramid

**Best for**: Breaking news, data-driven findings, strong conclusions

**Structure**:
1. **Lede**: The most important finding or conclusion (1 paragraph)
2. **Context**: Why this matters now (1-2 paragraphs)
3. **Evidence**: Supporting data, quotes, or documentation (3-4 paragraphs)
4. **Implications**: What follows from this (1-2 paragraphs)
5. **Kicker**: Forward-looking question or call to attention (1 paragraph)

**Rules**:
- Each paragraph is less important than the one before it
- A reader can stop after paragraph 3 and still have the story
- No surprises in the final third

**Example opening**: "Chile's pension system lost $2.3 billion last quarter. Here's what that means for every worker under 40."

---

### Pattern 2: Tension-Data-Resolution

**Best for**: Contested issues, policy debates, polarized topics

**Structure**:
1. **Lede**: The conflict or tension (1 paragraph)
2. **Side A**: Strongest argument for first position (2 paragraphs)
3. **Side B**: Strongest argument for opposing position (2 paragraphs)
4. **Data**: Evidence that transcends or reframes the debate (2-3 paragraphs)
5. **Resolution**: Synthesis or new frame (1-2 paragraphs)
6. **Kicker**: Unanswered question or next battleground (1 paragraph)

**Rules**:
- Present each side at its strongest (steel-man, not straw-man)
- Data section must introduce new information, not just arbitrate
- Resolution does not mean both-sidesism; it means a better frame

**Example opening**: "Everyone agrees the ports are congested. No one agrees whose fault it is. The answer lies in a spreadsheet no one wanted to read."

---

### Pattern 3: Narrative Arc

**Best for**: Human stories, profiles, investigative journeys

**Structure**:
1. **Lede**: A scene or moment (1 paragraph)
2. **Rising action**: Background and complications (3-4 paragraphs)
3. **Climax**: The revelation or turning point (1-2 paragraphs)
4. **Falling action**: Consequences and aftermath (2 paragraphs)
5. **Kicker**: Return to the human scale (1 paragraph)

**Rules**:
- The person or scene in the lede must reappear in the kicker
- Every paragraph must move time forward or deepen understanding
- No exposition dumps; distribute background across the arc

**Example opening**: "At 6:47 AM, Maria Vargas refreshed the government portal for the 47th time. The subsidy she needed to keep her bakery open had vanished into a server error."

---

### Pattern 4: Layered Explanation

**Best for**: Complex systems, technical topics, regulatory changes

**Structure**:
1. **Lede**: The surface-level phenomenon (1 paragraph)
2. **Layer 1**: The immediate mechanism (2 paragraphs)
3. **Layer 2**: The institutional context (2 paragraphs)
4. **Layer 3**: The historical or structural root (2 paragraphs)
5. **Synthesis**: How the layers connect (1 paragraph)
6. **Kicker**: What to watch next (1 paragraph)

**Rules**:
- Each layer must be comprehensible without the next
- Use analogies sparingly and precisely
- Never introduce a layer without explaining why it matters

**Example opening**: "The algorithm that determines your mortgage rate is not written by a banker. It is written by a team in San Francisco who have never been to your neighborhood."

---

### Pattern Selection Flowchart

```
Is the topic urgent and data-driven? -> Inverted Pyramid
Is the topic contested with strong voices on both sides? -> Tension-Data-Resolution
Is there a compelling human element at the center? -> Narrative Arc
Is the topic a system or mechanism that needs explaining? -> Layered Explanation
None of the above? -> Default to Inverted Pyramid with narrative lede
```
